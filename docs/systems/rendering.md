# Rendering

How the runtime draws scenes as the N64 does, and what's left. The runtime's other stand-ins are in [runtime](runtime.md).

## On-screen values

The N64 has no linear light. Its texels, vertex colours, tints and blends are the values it puts on screen, and the game turns the VI's gamma off (`osViSetSpecialFeatures` in `gfx.cpp`), so the emulator shows the framebuffer as it is. Godot works in linear light, where the same numbers come out differently:

- **Multiplying.** A texel times a vertex shade of 0.5 is half as bright on the N64. Treated as linear, the shade shows at about 73%.
- **Blending.** Godot blends in linear light. A surface at 10% over dark stone adds far more than 10% of its brightness, and black over the scene darkens about half as much as it should.

**Every float colour, alpha and volume becomes a byte the same way,** as 1.0 does it and `ColorBytes` does: multiplied, truncated toward zero (`trunc.w.s`, `trunc.w.d`; the code segment has no rounding instruction), and the low byte kept, so a negative wraps rather than clamps. What differs by site is the multiply's precision, which follows the original's literal: `255.0f` in single (`ColorBytes.Single`) for the sky, particles, HUD, shadows, lights and fades, and a `255.0` double (`ColorBytes.Double`) for the rain loop, the scene objects' view-cone alpha and the audio keys. Sums before the multiply that use a double literal (0.65, 0.15, 0.2, 0.35, 1.5) are worked in double too.

The scene export decodes vertex colours and untextured tints as sRGB, so they're correct glTF (`COLOR_0` is linear) and a viewer that multiplies in linear light comes out almost as the N64 does. The N64 material goes further and works in on-screen values itself.

**The runtime's 3D image holds on-screen values,** so Godot's blender mixes them as the N64's does. The N64 material writes its on-screen colour without turning it linear (`N64Material.OnScreenFramebuffer`), and the environment's colour correction is a table that decodes sRGB (`OnScreenOutput`). Godot applies colour correction after it encodes the image for the screen, so the two cancel and the screen gets the values as drawn. The sky's backdrop is a canvas that Godot decodes as it takes it in behind the scene, so a full-screen pass at the top of its layer encodes it first; with 8 bits a channel, neighbouring shades of the sky can merge near white. Everything translucent blends this way: water, the faded levels of detail, fire and the shadow (which darkens the ground to 0.68 of itself at its middle, the N64's 1 − 80/255). The editor still works in linear light (no `OnScreenFramebuffer` or `OnScreenOutput`), so its own overlays, the collision's colours, the markers and the grid, draw as they are, and its translucent draws blend as Godot's do.

## The N64 material

`N64Material` (in `AidynRenewed.Views`) replaces every material Godot's glTF import makes, in the runtime and in the editor's previews. It builds each from the glTF's own material, because Godot's import converts colours and samplers in ways the N64 doesn't.

- **The combiner.** `setStaticMode` sets textured draws to `TEXEL0 × SHADE`, then `× PRIMITIVE` in the second cycle, and untextured ones to `SHADE × PRIMITIVE` (combine modes `0x127E03, 0xFF0FF3FF` and `0xFFFE03, 0xFF0E79FF`). Alpha is texel alpha times prim alpha, or shade alpha times prim alpha: a textured draw ignores its vertices' alpha. The shader reads raw texels (no `source_color`), turns the vertex colour back into on-screen values and multiplies there. In the runtime it hands Godot the on-screen result, and in the editor the linear one.
- **The blender** (`Scene.SetRenderMode`, `0x80097618`, which sets every scene draw's render mode and prim colour). Prim alpha is the scene's alpha × (1 − the mesh tint's alpha) × (1 − the model's alpha), truncated to a byte: 0.1 is 25 (Gwernia Castle's window light), 0.5 is 127 (its sea). The draw is skipped when either alpha of the model or mesh is 1. Under 255 it's blended (`G_RM_AA_ZB_XLU_SURF2`, `0x1049D8`): an ordinary blend, `pixel × a + screen × (1 − a)`, depth tested and not written, whatever the texture. At 255, a texture with transparent texels (Borg1 flag 0x20) is blended if it's IA16, IA8 or RGBA32 and cut out otherwise (`G_RM_AA_ZB_TEX_EDGE2`, `0x113078`), and everything else is opaque (`G_RM_AA_ZB_OPA_SURF2`, `0x112078`). Every scene starts from the static state list at `0x800E4758` (`Scene.InitDrawState`, `0x80097300`), and each display list adds its own (`SetStaticMode`, `0x800973b8`).
- **Cut-outs are cut by coverage.** There's no alpha compare anywhere (`G_AC_NONE`, and no `G_SETBLENDCOLOR`): `CVG_X_ALPHA` multiplies the pixel's coverage, eight samples, by the filtered texel alpha, and the pixel is drawn while any of it is left, from alpha 32 of 255, with `ALPHA_CVG_SEL` and the VI softening what's left partly covered. So the port's alpha scissor cuts at an eighth (the glTF's `alphaCutoff`, `SceneGltf.CutOutAlpha`). Cut at half, foliage is thinner than the original's, and where a filter takes in a texture's transparent edge, such as the foot of the Overworld K08's tree wall (CI8 0xF7B in `aidyn:scene/0x1037`, whose T wraps past 2 there), the sky shows through where the original's doesn't.
- **The original's dither.** Translucent draws on the emulator look grainy: the RDP's colour dither (`G_CD_MAGICSQ`, a fixed 4 × 4 pattern) as it writes each pixel to the 16-bit framebuffer (5 bits a channel; `StartGfxList` sets it), with the VI's dither filter on (`osViSetSpecialFeatures(0x62)`: gamma off, divot off, dither filter on). The port draws at full precision and doesn't dither, so its blends are smooth.
- **Order.** The original draws a scene's display lists in its instructions' order, translucent ones where they fall, and sorts nothing. Godot draws translucent surfaces after opaque ones, sorted by distance, which can differ where two translucent surfaces overlap. Across scenes, the original draws a frame in this order (`RenderZones`, `0x8000d574`, and `HandleZoneEngineFrame`): the sky; each neighbour chunk in view, its scene and then its objects; the centre chunk's scene; the particles of emitters flagged for the first pass ([particles](#particles)); the bodies' shadows; bodies in water; the centre chunk's objects; translucent bodies in water; the other bodies; the chunks' second scenes, such as water (`RenderTransZones`, `0x8000dd44`); then the scene objects that are fading or flagged `Deferred` (`Zones.DrawDeferredScenes`, `0x8000dc98`); then every other emitter's particles (`ProcessAndRenderParticleHead`, `0x800a842c`), and the weather.
- **Texel centres.** The RDP's bilinear filter centres texel *k* on coordinate *k*, where a GPU's centres it on *k* + 0.5, so the material moves every coordinate half a texel before wrapping or clamping. Without it, a shape whose coordinates stop just inside a texture's edge blends in the far side's texels, wrapped round: Shadow Self Merge's pool of blood (IA16 0x2417, transparent in its first column and opaque in its last) gets a dark line along its edge. Cut-outs such as trees' and Oriana's path lights' edges take the same shift; side by side, nothing looks moved. The particles' and the UI's own shaders don't: a particle's texture is clear all round its quad's edge.
- **Mip levels.** `Scene.SetTextureTiles` (`0x80094104`) sets `G_TL_LOD` when the Borg1 has mip levels (header byte 6 at least 1) and `G_TL_TILE` when it hasn't, and `G_TD_DETAIL` or `G_TD_SHARPEN` by its flags 0x40 and 0x80, then a tile per level. About a fifth of the textures have none, the tree walls among them (CI8 0xF7B and 0xF7D), and the original draws those from the base level at every distance, so they alias as they shrink. The combiner never takes `TEXEL1` or `LOD_FRACTION`, so a texture with levels is drawn from one level a pixel, not blended between two. The port mipmaps every texture, trilinear, which smooths far textures the original lets shimmer; whether to keep that is the user's to choose ([roadmap](../roadmap.md)).
- **Seams between meshes.** Two meshes meant to meet often miss by a hair, and the original shows the background through the gap: in the Overworld K08, J08's scene (`0x100C`) and K08's (`0x1037`) each model their side of a corner of tree walls and the path beneath, 0.004 apart. On the console each chunk's vertices go through their own matrix, in fixed point, and are rasterised at 320 × 240, so pixels on the line fall to neither mesh and the sky shows in dashes. The port has the same gap and draws it as the GPU does: under a pixel wide, a broken line without antialiasing and a faint unbroken one with it. Showing the console's crack would take snapping vertices to the console's grid, and closing it welding the seams: the user's to choose ([roadmap](../roadmap.md)).
- **Mirrored models** <a id="mirrored-models"></a>show their insides. The RSP culls a triangle by the way it winds on screen, whatever its matrix, so a model whose world matrix mirrors it (an odd number of negative scales up its transforms) has its front culled and its back drawn. Godot turns the culling round for a mirrored instance. The importer marks the scenes whose transforms can mirror a model, at rest or as an animation scales them (the glTF's `mirrors`: cinematic shots, such as Shadow Self Merge's Shadow, and actors), and `MirroredMeshes` draws a mesh whose world matrix mirrors it with copies of its one-sided materials that cull the other side (`N64Material.Mirrored`), checked each time the scene is posed.
- **Shade** is the vertex colour on unlit display lists. On lit ones it's the RSP's lighting (see [bodies' light](#bodies-light) below), which the runtime and the editor work out per vertex (`N64Material.RspLighting`). Unlit meshes are exported without `NORMAL`, as the N64 doesn't light them, so Godot generates its own, which don't reliably face up: anything that reads unlit geometry's normals, such as a decal's angle fade, needs them exported. The Inspector lights bodies from the sky over the chunk its game stands in, at the game's time of day (`EditorGame.Sky`), with the body ambient and the sun alone (`N64Material.SetSkyLight`): no point lights, and its own Godot lights light nothing of the N64 material's.
- **How shade is interpolated.** The RDP walks a triangle's edges with the shade's increments a pixel across and a line down, which the RSP works out from the vertices on the screen: Gouraud in on-screen values, straight across the screen, with no perspective correction (only the texture's coordinates are corrected). The fog's amount rides in shade alpha and is interpolated the same way. The N64 material does the same: it turns each vertex's colour into on-screen values in the vertex shader, and passes the shade and the fog multiplied by the vertex's clip-space w, with that w, dividing again per pixel, which undoes Godot's perspective-correct interpolation (Godot has no `noperspective` varyings). Interpolating in linear light instead makes every blend between a bright vertex and a dark one brighter and less tinted, which shows most on Oriana's hut's floor (MiscInteriors E03, scene `0x1491`).
- **Prim** is the scene's tint, `SceneData.colorFloats`, times the mesh tint for untextured draws (`Scene.SetRenderMode`). Every chunk scene and scene object gets `gMainColor` (`SetSceneColors`), which the sky sets (below). In `Zones.SetSceneColors` (`0x8000c298`), `RenderVoxelScenes` passes a scene object's tint word (+0x64) only when its `Tint` flag is set, and when the whole word, alpha and all, isn't 0, each channel is `(gMainColor + tint) >> 1`, an even average truncated, with nothing clamped or weighted, then scaled by the screen fade's brightness (`0x8004d220` with `ScreenFade.Alpha`, 1 when nothing fades), which the untinted path doesn't do. Oriana's path lights in I08 are tinted this way. Bodies get white and are lit instead (`Bodies.SetLights`, `0x80013798`; scene objects' actors likewise), so actors' meshes are marked as bodies.
- **Instance uniforms are `Vector4`s, not `Color`s.** Godot turns a `Color` set on an instance uniform linear, so the tint (`N64Material.SetObjectTint`) and prim (`TextureScrolls`' copies) are set as `Vector4`. Likewise `Resource.Duplicate()` resets a `Color` set on a `vec4` shader parameter to the uniform's default.
- **Fog** is per vertex, as the RSP computes it. The amount is the depth buffer's `z / w` for the original's planes, on the 0–1000 scale `gSPFogPosition` takes (0 is `z / w` of −1, 1000 is 1), between the fog's start and end. Under Draw Distance's Extended, in trek, it's worked out from the distance to the eye in place of the depth, against planes moved out over the 5 × 5 ([draw distance](#draw-distance)). Draws blend toward the fog colour in on-screen values before the pixel is written, as `G_RM_FOG_SHADE_A` does. The editor leaves the fog off (below). With trek's planes of 1 and 180 and no fog in the weather, the fog runs from 996 to 1000, which is from about 105 units to the far plane. The weather's fog moves the start in, except while a cut-scene plays ([weather](weather.md#the-fog)). The runtime's follow camera stops at 180, as the original's does.
  - *The planes are the frame's.* `RenderZones` (`0x8000d574`) sets the zone engine's camera scene's planes before it loads its projection (`Scene.SetClipPlanes`, `0x8009e234`, into the camera's Borg3, which `Borg5Scene.EvaluateNodes`'s `guPerspectiveF` reads, both × 16 with the eye): 0.1 and 125 while `Game.Mode` is 0xC, a cut-scene, else 1 and 180, battles included. Every scene in the frame, scenery, scene objects and bodies, is drawn under that one projection (`Zones.SetSceneColors` sets each one's flag 0x40, `Scene.UseZoneCamera`, `0x8009e444`, so it keeps the projection loaded), so the fog's 996 is about 21 units out in a cut-scene. The port measures the fog against the same planes (`SkyDirector.Planes`, which `SkyView` hands the material), whatever the Godot camera's own are.
  - *Bodies are fogged as the scenery is.* `Scene.SetRenderMode` (`0x80097618`) turns the fog on when the scene's flag 0x100 is set: `G_FOG` in the geometry mode, `G_SETFOGCOLOR` from `+0x214` (`Scene.SetFogColor`, `0x8009e5b4`), `gSPFogPosition` as 128000 / (max − min) and (500 − min) × 256 / (max − min) from `+0x20c` and `+0x210` (`Scene.SetFogRange`, `0x8009e61c`), and `G_RM_FOG_SHADE_A` in the blender's first cycle. `Bodies.SetLights` (`0x80013798`) colours each body's scene through `Zones.SetSceneColors` as the chunks' are, so a body takes the scenery's fog range and colour, in trek and on a stage alike; a cast's glow is what clears it ([combat](combat.md#what-spells-look-like)).
- **Wrapping.** <a id="wrapping"></a>Borg2 materials clamp, repeat or mirror S and T separately (faces are often half a texture, mirrored), which a `BaseMaterial3D` can't, so the shader wraps each axis itself. 1.0 sets the tile as each list's material loads its texture (`Scene.SetTextureTiles`, `0x80094104`): S is `G_TX_CLAMP` unless flag 0x1000 is set, with `G_TX_MIRROR` added for 0x4000 (`0x8009422C`–`0x80094240`), and T the same by 0x2000 and 0x8000; each mask is the level's size's log2 (`0x80093844`, by halving) and the tile's size is the level's less one (`G_SETTILESIZE` at `0x80094640`). So a clamped axis clamps at the texture's edge, and a wrapped one repeats or mirrors at its size, which the importer writes as the glTF's sampler (`SceneGltf.Wrap`) and the shader follows. A texture coordinate past the 16 bits a vertex holds is the model's own, wrapped round in the data, and streaks the same on both ([combat](combat.md#the-arenas): arenas 24 and 27's floor). One case differs: a mirror bit without its wrap bit is `G_TX_MIRROR | G_TX_CLAMP`, which the RDP clamps to the tile before mirroring, so it never mirrors, where the port mirrors. One list in the game has it (Borg2 8547, scenes `0x21BF` and `0x2CC3`, T), and its coordinates stay within its 32-texel textures but for three quarters of a texel, so it doesn't show; it's left.
- **Globals.** The tint, the fog colour and the fog's range are global shader parameters (`n64_tint`, `n64_fog_color`, `n64_fog`), untinted and unfogged until the runtime sets them from its sky (`N64Material.SetSky`). The Inspector leaves them so, since its cameras aren't trek's, and sets only the light.

## The sky's colours

Every frame, `Sky::RenderSky` sets five colours from the sky gradient of the terrain the player is in, and the scenery is multiplied by the first. `AidynRenewed.Core.Sky` ports it, the sky director steps it, and the runtime's `SkyView` pushes it to the shaders.

- **Where it lives.** The core's `SkyDirector` holds the whole sky: the gradient that's up and the one it's changing to (`SkyState`), the colours last worked out (`Colors`), the sun, moon and stars (`SkyObjects`), the clouds (`Clouds`) and the lens flare (`LensFlare`), and it decodes the gradients from the plugins' PNGs itself (`PngImage`). There's one, on the zone engine (`ZoneEngine.Sky`), which trek's world and every stage made from it share, as the original's `HandleZoneEngineFrame` draws the one sky for each. `TrekLoop.Enter` gives it the player's chunk every tick. The session (`GameSession.Sky`) chooses again at once as a cut-scene's stage or a battle's arena is built and as trek's comes back (`ClearSky`), steps it after the mode ticks (`Step`: the change of sky, the clouds, the flare, then the colours) but not while the world is held or on the tick trek fades out into a cut-scene or a battle, and works the colours out again through a teleporter and as trek comes back (`Apply`). The stars twinkle once a 30 fps frame in every mode (`StepTwinkle`), and a battle sets the fade (`Dim`) from the spells' visuals each tick. The runtime only shows it: `SkyView` pushes the colours to the N64 material's globals each frame, `SkyBackdropView` and `LensFlareView` draw it from the camera's interpolated transform, and the runtime hands back the two things only the drawing knows, for the lens flare: where the sun's middle landed (`SkyObjects.SunMiddle`) and whether the sky shows there (`SkyDirector.SkyShows`). The sun's and moon's heights and the moon's phase are the calendar's (`GameClock.SunHeight`, `MoonHeight`, `MoonPhase`), which the moon's flags and the spells read too.
- **The gradients** are eight Borg8s, 12 × 37 RGBA32 (`CodeTable.SkyGradients`, read from the code segment). Rows 0–31 are the sky from top to bottom. Rows 32–36 are the tint, sky, fog, cloud and sun colours. There's a column every two hours from midnight, and `Sky::GenerateSky` blends between the two columns either side of the time of day, wrapping from 22:00 to midnight.
- **The tint** (`gMainColor`) is `tint row × 0.65 + 0.15`, plus a fifth of the sky colour, clamped and truncated to bytes (`Sky::SetColors`, `SkyColors.At`, `0x8001cee0`), the grey and the fifth worked in double and cast to float, then scaled by the screen's brightness and the sky's slower fade in turn (`Sky.Brightness`, `0x800D9518`, which `Sky.FadeBrightness`, `0x8001DE88`, moves: a spell's cast dims it to half and back; the port's `SkyBrightness`, which also scales the backdrop). The tint row is grey, darkest at midnight. Lens flare darkens it by half its value while the camera faces the sun ([the sky's objects](#the-skys-objects)). The time of day is one multiplier over all the scenery.
- **Which sky.** `BorgMaps::GetMapTerrain` picks a sky number by terrain in a switch, which the importer carries as a fact on terrain records. Terrain types 6, 8, 9, 11 and 13–16 don't draw the gradient: the background is black, there's no weather, but the colours still come from it. Every new chunk the player enters chooses again (`Sky::SetBackgroundType` with 600), which fades to a different sky over 600 ticks. Choosing again restarts the fade, and choosing the sky that's up abandons it. Into or out of a sky that isn't drawn, between two that aren't, and as trek's world is built, the sky changes at once (`LoadGameBorgScenes`, `0x8000b05c`, `SkyState.Enter`). Rain and snow bring the rain sky and hold it against entering chunks ([weather](weather.md#the-rain-sky)).
- **The clock.** A new game starts at noon on day 2 of week 6 (`InitGameGlobals`), and trek time runs 72 times as fast as real time, so a day takes 20 minutes. `--time <hours>` starts at another time, and the overlay shows it.
- **The backdrop.** Behind the scenery, `Sky::GenerateGradient` draws rows 0–31 of the gradient down the original's 320 × 240 screen a line at a time (`GetVRes` is always 240). `SkyBackdrop` ports it and `SkyBackdropView` draws it, on a canvas layer the environment shows as the background, scaled to the window's height. The gradient spans 200 lines, from row 1/200 of the way down at the top of the screen to its foot at line 200 when the camera looks level. Looking down moves it up by sin(y) × 240 lines, where y is the height of the look's unit vector (the sine of a sine). Looking up doesn't move it down. Below the foot the screen takes the foot's colour, and looking down so far that no line is left leaves it black. It isn't projected, so it only roughly meets the scenery's horizon, and the far plane hides the difference.
- **The projected dome.** Under `SkyPlacement.Projected`, the default and the user's choice, the runtime projects the gradient as a dome instead (`SkyBackdrop.DomeFraction`, drawn per pixel by a shader in `SkyBackdropView`): each pixel takes the gradient's line for the elevation of the way through it, so its rows stay fixed against the scenery, as the sun and clouds do. Each elevation keeps the line the original gives it down the middle of the screen at the follow camera's usual look (14.5° down): the horizon is 0.52 of the way down the gradient, its top 17.1° up, above which the sky is its top colour, and its foot 18.5° under the horizon. At that pitch the two placements agree within a level or two; as the follow camera pitches (0°–45°), the dome keeps the sky from sliding against the scenery and the sun. `--sky original` or F6 draws the original's. The weather's fog fades the backdrop to the fog colour, lens flare lifts it, and lightning fills the screen white in its place for a frame ([weather](weather.md#the-fog), [lightning](weather.md#lightning-and-thunder)). The canvas passes on-screen values through.

## The sky's objects

An outdoor sky draws, in order: the gradient, then the stars, the sun and the moon (`Skyobjects::Render`), then the clouds (`Clouds::Render`), then the scenery, then the lens flare (`Lensflare::Render`), then the HUD. The tables come from the ROM on the sky record, `aidyn:sky/0` (see the [importer](importer.md)). `AidynRenewed.Core.Sky` has `SkyObjects`, `Clouds` and `LensFlare`, which the sky director keeps and steps; `SkyBackdropView` draws the first two behind the scene and `LensFlareView` the flare over it. All are sprites on the 320 × 240 screen, texel × colour with texel alpha × alpha, blended normally (`borg8DlistInit` with flag 6: combine `0xFC119623 FF2FFFFF`, `TEXEL0 × PRIMITIVE` for both, and `G_RM_XLU_SURF2`), with bilinear filtering. Godot's 2D blends in on-screen values too.

- **Placement isn't projected.** Each object has two angles, a height and an azimuth. From the camera's look, x is 160 plus 300 × the sine of the azimuth against the look's heading, and y is 120 plus 300 × the sine of the elevation against the look's height. Perspective puts the scenery at 289.7 × the tangent instead (120 / tan 22.5°, for the Borg3 cameras' 45°). The two agree in the middle of the screen, but toward its sides the scenery turns up to about 30% faster than the sky, so the clouds, sun, moon and stars drag along with the camera. The azimuth turns half round while the height is between 90° and 270°, so an object carries on over the top. The elevation is sin(height) × 90°, not the height, so the sun is drawn at about 64° at 09:00 while it lights from 45°. The sprite's top-left corner goes at the point, not its middle.
- **The projected placement** is the default (`SkyPlacement.Projected`; `--sky original` or F6 for the original's), the user's choice. It gives each sprite a direction (its azimuth and elevation) and projects it as the scenery is projected (`SkyScreen.Project`: a 45° pinhole, pitch and all), so the sky stays pinned against the scenery at any pitch, like a skybox. Each sprite is a patch of sky: laid flat, facing the camera, a unit away in its direction, kept upright against the horizon, and sized so it's drawn as the original draws it when the camera looks straight at it (`SkyPatch`). Its corners are projected like the scenery and it's drawn as a 4 × 4 grid of triangles, so the texture follows the perspective. The sky reads the camera's interpolated transform, as the scenery is drawn from, not the last tick's.
- **The sun**'s height is the time of day × 360° − 90°, so it's on the horizon at 06:00 and 18:00, and its azimuth is 90°: it rises in +X, as its light does. It's drawn in the sky's sun colour at alpha 250, at scale 1 (64 pixels). Where its middle lands is kept for the lens flare, and kept while it isn't drawn.
- **The moon**'s images are IA8 with every intensity at full, so each is a white disc shaped by its alpha, with no surface. Its height is twice (time of day × 360° + 90°), so it goes round twice a day, and it sways 40° either side of its azimuth of 0. Its image is its phase (`World::set_moonPhase`) on a cycle of four weeks, with the first two counted as waxing, though the images show it waning then: full on days 0–2, a quarter on 3–7, a crescent on 9–13, new on 14–16, a crescent on 17–21 and a quarter on 23–27, with days 8 and 22 turning at noon. A new game starts under a new moon (day 16), whose image is faint (alpha at most 85).
- **The stars** show only while the sun is down, from 18:00 to 06:00, at the sine of how far down it is × their alpha, so they come out as it sets, at scale 0.19 (`Skyobjects::Init` sets it over the table's 0.2). Each frame each takes a random grey from 160 to 255, so they twinkle. The port twinkles them once a 30 fps frame ([ADR 0017](../adr/0017-the-core-steps-every-tick.md)), in every mode, while an outdoor sky is up and the sun is down (`SkyObjects.Twinkle`), every star in the table's order whether it's on the screen or not, from a stream of its own (`RandomStreams.Twinkle`, the original's generator from the boot seed, which a save keeps), so the sounds' and lights' cosmetic stream doesn't depend on the sky.
- **The clouds** are points on a plane within 750 of the camera, drawn in the sky's cloud colour. A cloud's nearness is 1 − distance / 750, and it's drawn 240 × nearness above the horizon line (120 + 300 × the look's height), at nearness + 0.2 times its size, with alpha × √nearness; it's centred by nearness, not by its scale, so slightly off. Projected, a cloud is a direction: its heading on the plane, at the elevation the original draws it at when the camera looks straight at it, a sine of 0.8 × nearness, from the horizon at the plane's edge to 53° up over the camera. The plane goes with the camera, so walking and turning don't move a cloud against the scenery, only the wind does. The game takes the way to the camera with X and Z swapped against the sky objects' azimuths, which only mirrors the layout. The wind ([weather](weather.md#the-wind), `Weather.DriftWind`) blows them 4 × its velocity a frame, which the port spreads over the frame's ticks, and one that goes past 750 comes back in on the far side. They fade in and out as the cloud cover passes each one's threshold, but nothing changes the cover from 1, so all show.
- **The weather's fog** fades the sun by its thickness, and the moon, stars and clouds faster ([weather](weather.md#the-fog)).
- **The lens flare** shows while the sun's middle is on screen, 10 pixels inside the edges, and nothing hides it. It fades in by 10 a frame up to 50 and out the same, over 5 frames. Its eight pieces are drawn along the line from the middle of the screen through the sun, at their distance along it, at their scale × the sun's distance from the middle / 80 up to 1, and not at all at 0.2 or less, in their colours at alpha fade × the tint's red. Within 80 of the middle it also sets `lensFlareVal`, the tint's red × (1 − distance / 80) × 0.5 × fade / 50, which next frame lowers the tint by half of it and lifts the gradient by all of it. The original tests the depth buffer under the sun's middle at the end of each frame, for the next. The port sends a ray through that point against the collision, out to the far plane, which catches the ground and buildings but not leaves.

## Bodies' light

The sky lights bodies and animated scene objects; scenery it only tints. `AidynRenewed.Core.Sky.SkyLight` has the pieces, and the N64 material lights each vertex from global parameters that `SkyView` sets each frame from the sky director's colours. The zone engine starts each frame of lights with the director's sun and moon (`ZoneEngine.BeginLightFrame`).

- **The RSP's lighting** is the ambient plus each light's colour times the cosine between the vertex's normal and the way to the light, where that's positive, clamped to 1, in on-screen values, per vertex. The game puts the camera in the projection matrix (`gsAnimationDataMtx` loads it with `G_MTX_PROJECTION`), so the modelview is the model alone and the lights are in world space.
- **Three kinds of lit mesh** (`N64Material.Lighting`). Bodies, the player among them, get white prim, an ambient of half `gSkyColor` truncated to bytes, and the sun (`Bodies.SetLights`, `0x80013798`). Scene objects' actors get white prim, `gMainColor` as ambient, and the sun, unless they're fullbright, when they're tinted like scenery. Scenery's lit meshes keep the white ambient `Scene::SetLightData` gives every scene and no other light, so they come out as the tint alone. Bodies and actors also get the [point lights](#point-lights) that reach them.
- **The sun** is `gSunColor` from the direction `Skyobjects::UpdateSun` works out: at an angle of the time of day × 360° − 90° about the Z axis, turned by the sun's 90° in the sky objects' table. It rises in +X at 06:00, is overhead at noon and sets in −X at 18:00, and lights from below at night, when the gradient's sun colour is dark. Only an outdoor sky has it; in caves (`SkyType4`) bodies get the ambient alone.
- **The engine's lights reach every other vertex.** `SceneLights.Add` (`0x8009e838`) writes a light's colour into `colc`, the second of the two copies in `Light_t`, and zeroes `col` (bytes 0–3 zero, 4–6 the colour and 8–10 the direction, × 120), and the draw points the RSP straight at those lights (`BorgAnimDrawSceneRaw`, `0x80097b10`–`0x80097c44`, `G_MOVEMEM` of the scene's `+0x218` on, then the ambient at `+0x288`, whose `Scene.SetAmbient`, `0x8009eaa4`, fills both copies). F3DEX2 transforms and lights a `G_VTX`'s vertices two at a time from the load's first, whatever buffer slot it starts at: the first of each pair by `colc`, the second by `col`. So the sun, the moon and the point lights light a body's or a placed actor's 1st, 3rd, 5th … vertex of each load, and the others take the ambient alone; the triangles shade between the two. Running 1.0's microcode in an emulator of the RSP shows it: one light of 200 in `colc` alone, facing the normals, shades a load of 32 196, 0, 196, 0 …, and the same from slot 17. A scene's own lights (below) fill both copies, so they reach every vertex. It's why the sun lights a body unevenly, and why a target mark's dark side (combat's ambient near 0 under the moon's full cyan) shows only a little green ([combat](combat.md#an-attack)). The importer marks the vertices a lit list loads second of a pair (`N64Vertex.SecondOfPair`, from `F3dex2`, an S of 1 in `TEXCOORD_1`), and the N64 material's engine-lit kinds (`Lighting.Body` and `SceneObject`) give those the ambient alone. Side by side with the emulator, bodies in daylight and a marked rat's dark side look as the original's.
- **The moon** adds a light whenever it's up (`Sky.MoonDirection`'s y, `0x800D953C`, over 0, from 06:00 to noon and 18:00 to midnight) and the sky is outdoor, after the sun: from the moon's way (its height and its table azimuth, without the sway it's drawn with, `SkyObjects.UpdateMoon`, `0x8002A090`), cyan, (0, g, g) with g = trunc(200 × the moon's height × the screen's fade × the body's tint level) (`Bodies.SetLights`). Outside combat the level is 0, so it's black, but it still takes one of the body's four lights. The port does the same (`WorldLights.Moon`, `SkyLight.MoonDirection`).
- **The tint** (body `+0x75C` level, `+0x760` rate, `+0x764` colour), which only combat changes ([combat](combat.md#what-spells-look-like)): each frame `Bodies.Process` moves the level by its rate and, while either isn't 0, moves the ambient from half the sky's colour toward the colour by the level, × the screen's fade, clamped (`0x80012B1C`). The port's is `Combatant.Tint`, which the runtime hands the N64 material as the body's own ambient.
- **Point lights** add more: light map objects and dynamic lights (below), as directional lights from the body toward each.

## Point lights

Light map objects and the game's dynamic lights light bodies and the actors maps place, as directional lights worked out for each draw. `AidynRenewed.Core.Lights` ports them (`PointLight`, `WorldLights`, `SceneLights`, `DynamicLights`), and the runtime's `Game.LightBodies` hands each lit model its lights every tick, which the N64 material adds to the sun and ambient per vertex (`N64Material.SetLights`, up to four).

- **How many.** A scene has room for 7 lights and an ambient (`Scene.ResetLights`, `0x8009e778`). `SceneSetMaxDynamicDirLights` (`0x8009e7d0`) lowers it to 4 for bodies (`Bodies.Render`, `0x800140f0`) and 2 for actors a map places (`RenderVoxelScenes`, `0x8000cd3c`); nothing else calls it. The sun comes first while the sky is outdoor, then the moon while it's up, so a body outdoors has 3 for point lights, or 2 with the moon up, and an actor 1. Once the room is full, a new light takes the place of the dimmest one after the sun and moon by r² + g² + b² (`Scene.LightBrightness`, `0x8009e800`) if that's dimmer, and is dropped otherwise (`SceneLights.Add`, `0x8009e838`, which ignores the brightness byte it's passed). The colour goes in `colc` and the direction × 120 (above). The draw (`BorgAnimDrawSceneRaw`) hands the RSP the lights and the ambient, and empties the list.
- **What's lit.** A body (`Bodies.SetLights`, `0x80013798`) gets the sun, the moon, the dynamic lights (`Lights.AddDynamic`, `0x8004d9e0`), and then, only if it's one of the party's (`+0x70a`, which `InitGameTrek`, `0x80020f40`, sets on the player and combat's party spawn, `0x8005c474`, on the party's members, not their enemies), the light map objects that the grid cell under its centre lists in its own chunk (`WorldLights.ForBody`, `0x8004d878`). So in trek only the player is lit by map lights; wanderers get the dynamic lights alone. The Battle arenas have no light map objects. An actor a map places that isn't fullbright gets the sun, the dynamic lights and every light map object of its chunk (`MapObjects.SetActorLights`, `0x80013ac4`, through `Lights.AddZone`, `0x8004d9b0`), from where the map places it. Chunk scenery and plain scene objects get no light but the white ambient. The non-fullbright actors that maps place are nearly all torches (`0x186E`), whose meshes are unlit, so in practice map lights light the player alone. Lighting other bodies too may be worth doing one day; the port keeps the original's.
- **Each light** (`WorldLights.AddNear`, `0x8004d384`), if it's active (gated as the chunk loads, and never again, `VoxelObjects.RefreshActive`, `0x80017af8`):
  1. An occludable one (header 0x1000) is skipped if a sphere of 0.25 swept from the light to the body's centre hits a triangle that faces it, see-through ones (0x200) aside (`Collision.SweepBlocked`, `0x800a42e8`; the port's `SpherePhysics.Sweep`). The original sweeps the body's chunk alone; the port sweeps the loaded chunks.
  2. One the body's centre is no nearer than its radius to, in 3D, is skipped. Otherwise the falloff is (radius − distance) / radius: straight-line, no square.
  3. The first time in a frame something reaches the light, its colour moves on (below) and is scaled by that falloff, in place, in the light. Every time, it's scaled again by the screen fade's level (`ScreenFade.Alpha`, `0x800da0c8`: 1 clear, 0 black) and added, shining from the light, or from the camera's eye for those with header bit 0x1 (`Camera.Listener`, `0x800d829c`), whose distance is still from the light. So a second thing the light reaches in the same frame takes the first one's falloff, whatever its own distance, and the fade twice. The port does the same.
- **The colour** moves on once a frame, by the frame's ticks, and only in a frame something is in range, so a light nobody is near holds its colour. The port steps it per tick. Each kind gives a share of the way from its first colour to its second, and the colour is the first plus the difference times the share, truncated toward zero, channel by channel (`PointLight.Blend`, `0x8004cf88`, with `0x8004d220` for × the share):
  - *Static*: the first colour.
  - *Alternate*: the share goes up by ticks / (speed × 30). Past 1, the two colours change places and it wraps (`Lights.SwapColours`, `0x8004d17c`; `0x8004d150` takes off one period), so it fades there and back, speed × 30 ticks each way.
  - *Sine*: an angle goes up by 360 / (speed × 60) degrees a tick, wrapping at 360, and the share is (sin + 1) / 2: a cycle of speed × 60 ticks, from half way.
  - *Random*: the share moves toward a target by ticks / (speed × 60); within a step of it, it takes the target and draws another from 0 to 1 (`Rng.Float0To1`, `0x800933d4`, on the main stream; the port draws from the cosmetic stream, ADR 0006).
- **Dynamic lights** (`AllocDynamicLight`, `0x8004db1c`; `FreeDynamicLight`, `0x8004dbe0`; the manager at `0x800d8900`): 16 slots, each a light like a map object's built by `InitLight` (`0x8004ce80`), with no occlusion, and a life that counts down by each frame's ticks from the zone engine's frame (`DynamicLights.Step`, `0x8004dc74`), going out at 0. A new one goes in the slot numbered by how many are lit, not a free one: the original keeps a free list but never reads it, so after one goes out before a later slot's, the next new light takes the later one's place while it's lit. The port does the same. They go when the zone engine is freed (`FreeZoneEngine`); the port clears them when a teleport changes map set. In 1.0 only a trapped container's explosion adds any (`Containers.Explode`, `0x8000ffe4`): four fires where the container is, each drifting at random (speed 0.75) between dark red (0x60, 0, 0) and yellow within 5, the first for 600 ticks and the others for 60 (`Explosion.Fire`, `0x800174f8`), then a burst within 10 that fades from orange (0xFF, 0x80, 0) to black over its 120 ticks (alternate at speed 4, `Explosion.Burst`, `0x80016c00`). Spells, torches and scripts add none. Each comes with its emitter, a thrown fire's light following it ([map objects](map-objects.md#containers)).
- **In cut-scenes** the stage's zone engine draws as trek's does: the actors its maps place are lit by their chunk's lights (`MapObjects.SetActorLights`), and the cut-scene's actors are bodies, drawn by `Bodies.Render`'s first pass and lit by `Bodies.SetLights`. A cut-scene's bodies are cleared as they're made, so none, Alaron included, is the party's, and no light map object reaches them; the stage's dynamic lights start empty and nothing adds any. So in practice they have the sun alone. Lights that face the camera shine from the script camera's eye, and `RenderZones` takes the camera's aim in place of the player for the scene objects' draw distance and the grid cell whose objects it draws. The port lights the stage the same way (`Game.LightStage`, with the stage's own `WorldLights`, and `BodyView.Light`).
- **Nothing is drawn** for a light: no glow or flare. The flames are their own scene objects.

## A scene's own lights

A Borg5 can list Borg4 lights (see [scenes](../formats/scenes.md)). `BorgAnimDrawSceneRaw` (`0x80097a90`) lights a scene by them unless the scene has its engine-lit bit (`+0x1f4` & 0x8000, which `Scene.ResetLights` sets on bodies and the actors maps place) or bit 0x80 (whose only setter, `0x8009df6c`, is never called). Each light's colour is its Borg4's times its transform's scale, channel by channel (`+0x28`–`+0x30`, red by X, green by Y, blue by Z), each scale clamped to 0–1 and the product to 1, × 255 and truncated, into both copies (`0x80097d54`); light *k* shines along the third row of transform 1 + *k*'s matrix, × 90 into bytes. The ambient is tinted the same way by transform 1 + its index, which is the scene's first model node. Animations drive these scales, so a cinematic's lights brighten, dim and change colour. The last is the ambient: with *n* lights it hands the RSP *n* − 1 directional lights (at least 1), so with one alone that's the ambient, beside a black light (`0x800e4748`). A scene with none gets the white ambient (`0x800e4738`) and the black light.

- **The RSP's side** (F3DEX2 2.08's lighting overlay, ROM `0x800024e8`): on each vertex load it turns each light's direction into the model's space by the matrix and normalises it, so the 90 only sets the bytes' rounding (about 0.6°); the vertex's normal bytes are used as they are. Each light adds its colour × the dot of the two, ≤ (127/128)², and the ambient adds in full, clamped at 255: `shade = min(255, A + Σ floor(C · max(0, n · L) / 16384))`. The port's unit vectors make each light about 2% brighter than that, which it leaves. Below a matrix scale of about 0.01 the normalisation loses precision and dims, which no cinematic reaches.
- **Which scenes.** No chunk scene and no scene object's Borg5 has a light, so scenery always gets the white ambient. Most actors' scenes have lights, but bodies and the actors maps place have the engine-lit bit, so theirs are ignored for the sky's and the point lights. That leaves three users, which the port lights by their own:
  - *A cinematic's shots* ([cinematics](cinematics.md#drawing)).
  - *The spells' visuals*, their actors and lasting scenes in a battle, whose prim is the screen's fade (`Scene.SetTint`).
  - *The pause menu's scroll* (`0x0E63`): a dim blue (0.19, 0.18, 0.25) and a warm (0.83, 0.73, 0.62) directional light and a black ambient (`PauseScrollView`, [ui](ui.md#opening-and-closing)).
- **In the port**, the export puts each light transform's scale on its light node and its animation, and `OwnLights`, which the cinematic view and the spells' visuals share, tints each light and the ambient by it each frame and hands them to the N64 material (`N64Material.Lighting.Own`).

## Draw distance

There are no tiers. Each scene object has a radius, and each of its one or two levels of detail its own distance (`renderProx`), and `RenderVoxelScenes` draws and fades each level every frame. `AidynRenewed.Core.Scenes.SceneObjectFade` ports it, and the runtime's `ChunkStreamerView.Fade` applies it each frame (through `ChunkScenery.Fade`). Chunks' own scenes, the terrain, aren't faded: they're drawn out to the far plane, 180, and fogged from about 105, or further under Draw Distance's Extended (below).

- **From the player, not the camera.** Every distance is from the player's centre, in 3D (with no player, from the camera's aim). Godot's visibility ranges measure from the camera, which is several units behind the player, so they can't be used.
- **An object** isn't drawn from its radius out. The radius is usually the last level's distance, but not for containers: a container's object lists the closed, opened and destroyed models, of which only the first is a level of detail, so a chest's object has radius 30 and its model is drawn to 10.
- **A level of detail** is drawn from 2 short of the previous level's distance to its own. It fades out over its last unit, and fades in over its first. So as the player walks away, the next level fades in before this one fades out, and at any distance at most one is part-drawn. The last level fades out over its last unit and the object is gone, rather than popping.
- **The view.** On the ground, an object more than 37.5° off the camera's look fades out by 42.5°, linearly in the angle's cosine, measured from the camera to the object's position (the share as a float, × 255.0 in double, truncated: `0x8000c8cc`). At the follow camera's pitch, a 4:3 screen's corners are about 33° off the look, so it saves drawing what's behind and to the sides without being seen, except for a large object near the camera whose middle is out of view and whose edge isn't. The `DrawWhenNear` flag (0x4) skips this within half the object's radius. A wider window widens the cone by the tangent (`ViewCone.For`), so the fade stays as far outside the screen's edge as it does at 4:3. That's only what's drawn: Oriana's path lights go out by the original's cone whatever the window ([map-objects](map-objects.md#orianas-path-lights)).
- **Between the camera and the player.** An object that isn't `Solid` (flag 0x1), nearer the camera than the player and inside a cone from the player toward the camera, 0.35 of the way back wide, is drawn at 96 of 255 in the middle up to 255 at the cone's edge, whatever the look gave (the width is the distance × 0.35 in double, and the alpha is `trunc(across / width × 159) + 0x60`, truncated before the 0x60 is added: `0x8000c984`; the measure is `SceneObjectFade.Between`, `0x8000c430`). So bushes and small trees the camera looks through go see-through. Solid ones, such as most trees and the chests, stay.
- **How a faded level is drawn.** Its alpha is the lesser of the view's and its distance's. It's the scene's prim alpha (`SetSceneColors`), so texel alpha times it. Under 255, the scene is drawn after everything else, blended (`G_RM_AA_ZB_XLU_SURF2`, `0x1049D8` in `Scene.SetRenderMode`), testing depth but not writing it. The runtime sets Godot's per-instance `transparency`, which does the same, and the N64 material multiplies its own alpha into it. Cut-outs, the foliage and bushes, blend too, since the faded render mode has no alpha test, so texel alpha fades with the rest. Godot draws an alpha-scissor material at full alpha wherever it passes, whatever the instance's transparency, so a faded model's cut-out surfaces switch to a blended copy of their material (`N64Material.Faded`) and back when it's whole.
- **Bodies** have a draw distance of their own, not their actor's levels (`Bodies.Render`, `0x80013dcc`): the bodies' `+0x64` (`shadowDist`), 20 in trek and a cut-scene and 128 in combat (`BodyShadow.TrekDistance`, `CombatDistance`), from the camera's aim in 3D, fading out over its last 2 units (`PlayerShadowAlpha`, `0x800122c4`; `BodyShadow.BodyAlpha`). It also skips a body more than 42.5° off the camera's look on the ground (`Vec2.Cosine` from the eye, `0x800d829c`, of the aim and the body, against `cosf` of `0x800cdbd8`) unless it's within 5 of the aim on the ground (100 for entity `0xDC3`, `0x800cdbdc`, `0x800cdbe0`), and runs a test of the body's requests (`+0x14` against `+0x1A`, compared with `+0x1C`) first that isn't settled here. The runtime fades bodies by the distance alone (`BodyView.Fade`); what the cone skips is off the screen anyway. In trek and cut-scenes Object Distance scales the 20, and their shadows' with it, up to 30 at most (`ObjectDistance.Bodies`): past 30 from the aim a body neither moves nor animates ([wanderers](wanderers.md), `Body.StepDistance`), so drawing further would show wanderers stopped mid-stride and a body spawned over a hole hanging where it stopped falling ([scripts](scripts.md)). So 2x and 3x both draw bodies to 30. A battle's 128 isn't scaled.
- **An actor's particles show and fade with it, the user's choice** (Oct 24, 2026). 1.0 steps and draws every emitter's particles whether the actor it's on is drawn or not: neither `ProcessAndRenderParticleHead` (`0x800a842c`) nor `ParticleHead.Draw` (`0x800a92fc`) tests a distance or the actor, and particles aren't fogged, so they reach the far plane, 180. So a Fire Elemental's or a Lava Hound's flames show from well beyond the 20 its body is drawn to. The port draws an actor's particles at the alpha its model was last shown at, and none while it isn't drawn (`IParticleAnchor.Alpha`, set through `ActorParticles.Alpha` by `Level.Show`, which a body's model and a scene object's actor both go through), so they fade in and out with it over the same 2 units, and go with it when a cut-scene hides an actor or a freed body fades. Hidden, they go on stepping as 1.0's do, which costs nothing as they were stepped anyway, so a fire is already burning when it comes into view. Code's particles, the weather's and a trapped container's explosion, belong to no actor and are drawn as before, as are a spell's visual's, whose actor isn't faded.
- **Further than the original** is three settings on the options menu's Enhancements page, Draw Distance, Object Distance and Model Detail ([ui](ui.md#options); `Settings.ExtendedDrawDistance`, `ObjectDistance` and `NearestDetail`, or `--draw-distance extended`, `--object-distance <scale>` and `--detail nearest` for one run). They're the runtime's alone and aren't among what the core is handed ([ADR 0023](../adr/0023-the-core-is-handed-its-settings-each-tick.md)): putting the path lights out is the game's behaviour, which a save keeps, so it goes by the original's distances whatever the settings (`LoadedMap.PutOutDistances`), and what's loaded beyond the 3 × 3 is scenery the game never sees.
  - *Draw Distance's Extended* (`Core.Scenes.DrawDistance`), in trek, draws the Overworld's 5 × 5 chunks round the player, where the original loads the 3 × 3 (`Core.Chunks.SceneryRing`, which the runtime holds; [runtime](runtime.md#loading-chunks)). The 16 outer chunks are scenery only: their scenes and their scene objects, shown by the objects' flag gates and the memory that keeps a put-out light out (`LoadedMap.Scenery`), read without changing either, with no collision, sounds, lights, wanderers or map objects at work, and loading them isn't a map load, so what map objects remember doesn't age by them ([map objects](map-objects.md#a-chunks-objects-at-run-time)). A test plays a game with the ring and without and finds them the same (`SceneryRingTests`). The planes the fog is measured against move out from 1 and 180 to 5/3 and 300 (`DrawDistance.FarScale`): 300 is 1.2 times the 5 × 5's half-width from a chunk's middle, as 180 is the 3 × 3's, so what lies past the loaded chunks is as deep in the fog as the original's is, and the fog starts about 175 out, inside the 200 the 5 × 5 always reaches. The weather's fog pulls the planes back toward the original's as it thickens, to them at its thickest, 0.75 ([weather](weather.md#the-fog)): what's beyond can't be seen through it anyway. The frame is cut at a sphere about the eye at the planes' far, where the fog ends, and the fog goes by the distance from the eye (`N64Material.RadialParameter`), so turning the camera brings nothing in or out at the screen's corners, as the original's flat far plane does; the Godot camera's own far plane is twice the sphere's radius, which holds it at any window's corners. Only the N64 material is cut: the particles, the shadows and the sky have their own shaders, and none is drawn that far. The ring's actors start no emitters, as the particles' pool is shared with what's near and they're drawn only within their levels of detail, nearer than the ring. Off the Overworld, Extended keeps the 3 × 3 and the planes of 1 and 180, cut at a sphere of 180 and fogged by distance; interiors and the towns' sets are walled in. A cut-scene's stage, a battle's arena and the cinematics keep the original's planes and its flat cut: a cut-scene's fog of 0.1 and 125 takes everything past about 21 units, and the arenas are a single chunk.
  - *Stand-ins for the land round a chunk.* 29 Overworld chunks' scenes reach 100 to 330 past their own cell, with a rough sheet of the land round them (I19's runs across the beach to K19 and beyond; 118 others reach 16 or less, the ordinary overlap at the seams). In the original a stand-in shows only beyond the loaded chunks or over a neighbour, where the art keeps it under the real ground: two chunks two apart are drawn together only when the centre is between them, on either side of the camera, and the view a neighbour is drawn by ([scrolling textures](#scrolling-textures)) seldom takes in both. Under Extended the 5 × 5 puts real ground under stand-ins two and more from their chunks, which rise through it in places (I19's stands up to 3 above K19's sand and buries a stump there). So under Extended, on the Overworld's 5 × 5 (elsewhere Extended keeps the 3 × 3, drawn as the original's), a chunk's own scenes aren't drawn over a cell two or more from their own whose scenery is drawn: each scene's meshes carry their cell (`N64Material.SetChunkCell`, set as `ChunkStreamerView` shows the chunk), and the square of drawn cells is a global (`N64Material.SetCells`, from `ChunkStreamerView.DrawnCells` each frame), which the fragment shader discards by. What's past the drawn cells, and over neighbours, is drawn as before. Hiding a sheet over a neighbour too, where that neighbour has a chunk but isn't drawn (the edge of the 5 × 5), on the grounds that the art only ever puts real ground there, was tried and left out (Oct 24, 2026): it changed nothing to see at P18 or K08, and from Q19 looking east it punched ragged holes through the far sea, which only S18's sheet over T18 and T19 covers. Under Original the port draws every loaded neighbour whatever the view, so a stand-in can show over the chunk two from it while the player stands between them, where the original's view test would mostly leave it out; that's unchanged. **Trap:** `chunk_cell` is the material's 16th instance uniform, Godot's most; another needs one packed with it.
  - *Object Distance* (Original, 2x, 3x) multiplies each object's radius and each level's distance, and the fades keep their widths. Where an empty level gives way to one that draws something, the object vanishes as the player nears it, and there the data's own distance is kept at any scale, the user's choice (`SceneObjectFade.Boundary`), so Oriana's path lights fade out from 4 to 3 at every setting. An object shows to its scaled distance only while its chunk is loaded and the fog hasn't taken it. Under Draw Distance's Original the 3 × 3 is loaded at least 100 out and the fog starts about 105 out, so about 1.4× is as far as most objects show on the Overworld: 2x reaches its distance for the objects of radius 50 or less (2,950 of the Overworld's 3,343), and the rest stop at the 3 × 3's edge. Under Extended the 5 × 5 is loaded at least 200 out and the fog starts about 175 out: 2x reaches its distance for all but 7 objects (radius over 100), and 3x for the objects of radius 66 or less (3,030 of them), the rest fading into the fog toward the cut at 300; what's past about 175 is drawn in the fog's start.
  - *Together*: Original and Original is the original. Extended with Object Distance's Original draws the 5 × 5's terrain and each object to the original's distance, so the outer ring shows its terrain and its largest objects. Extended with 2x or 3x draws the objects out with the terrain.
  - *Model detail's Highest* (`ObjectDistance.NearestDetail`) is the port's own. It works out each level's alpha as the original does and draws the nearest level that draws anything, at the greatest of the alphas of the levels that draw something, and nothing else (`SceneObjectFade.Alphas`). So a tree shows its first level from 0 out to where its last ends. A level draws nothing when every draw of its scene is one the game skips, as the export skips them (`SceneModel.Empty`). Only Oriana's path lights have one: their first level (`0x1910`) is an untextured grey quad whose Borg2 (`0x190F`) has the hidden bit, so under Highest they're drawn as their billboard and fade out from 4 to 3 as the player nears them, as 1.0's do.

## Billboards

A Borg5 transform with flag 0x200 is a card the game turns to the camera (see [scenes](../formats/scenes.md)). `AidynRenewed.Core.Scenes.Billboard` ports the matrix, and the runtime's `Billboards` applies it.

- **What the game draws.** Only the transform's world position and its scale times the scene object's survive. The card rolls by the transform's `rot.z`, turns so its +Z points at the camera's position, not along the way it looks, and is scaled along the world's axes (`guAlignF`). So it tilts back as the camera looks down on it, and it can roll as the camera passes overhead.
- **In the runtime.** The export puts a facing node between the billboard's node and its card. The runtime makes the facing node top-level and, every frame, sets it to the billboard's world position, turned and scaled as the game does, from the camera's interpolated position. Animations still move and scale the billboard's node and roll the card, and the facing node reads them.

## Scrolling textures

Water and fire scroll: the game shifts the texture's bitmap sideways each time a scene loads it (see [textures](../formats/textures.md)). `AidynRenewed.Core.Scenes.TextureScroll` ports the counter, and the runtime's `TextureScrolls` applies it. No Borg5 uses the scene-level kind of animated texture, which the game would crash on.

- **The shader** takes how far the image has moved as a share of its width (`scroll`), and subtracts it from S after the material's own clamping or mirroring, then samples with repeat. That wraps the image round inside the texture as the game's shift does. It moves in whole texels, as the original's does, so it steps rather than glides.
- **Each copy of a scene has its own.** As in the original, each copy of a scene gets its own copy of each scrolling texture, so the runtime gives it its own materials for them (`TextureScrolls.Attach`); a shared one would move once for every copy drawn.
- **See-through ones**, mostly water, draw through a see-through tint, whose alpha is the material's prim alpha, set as a `Vector4` (see the instance-uniform trap under [the N64 material](#the-n64-material)).
- **When it moves.** Once a frame at 20 fps (every 3 ticks, `Cadence.TextureFrameTicks`), each copy of a scene the original would draw loads its scrolling textures, which moves them on. A scene that isn't drawn keeps its place. 20 is the user's choice: the console's average, a middle between spots that chug (the fire and gore outside Gwernia, about 15 fps) and interiors that run well. The original draws the centre chunk's two scenes always, and a neighbour's when the camera's view crosses it (`ChunkView`, from `ChunkView.Drawn`, `0x8000d270`) or the player's chunk's map has it drawn whatever the view (`MapRecord.DrawnNeighbours`). The view is a kite of five lines on the ground, 200 long, straight ahead and 42.5° either side. The runtime counts the scroll this way (`ChunkStreamerView.DrawnScenes`), and in the chunks it draws, a scene object's levels of detail scroll while they're drawn at all.
- **Neighbours drawn whatever the view.** `RenderZones` (`0x8000d574`) walks the eight neighbours in its table's order (`0x800CD700`: row and column −1 −1, +1 −1, −1 +1, +1 +1, then 0 −1, 0 +1, −1 0, +1 0) with a bit that starts at 1 and doubles, and draws a neighbour, its scene and its objects, when the view crosses it or that bit is set in byte 0x1B of the player's chunk's Borg9 (`Zones.PlayerMap`, `0x800d8288`). `RenderTransZones` (`0x8000dd44`) does the same for the second scenes, from a table of the same eight then the centre (`0x800CD83C`), whose bit has run out of the byte by then, so the centre's second scene is drawn only when the view crosses the centre chunk too. That always happens in chunks 100 across, and only those have second scenes, so it never shows. The import carries the byte as each map's `DrawnNeighbours`, named by compass (rows run east, columns south); only Overworld chunks set it. Nothing else in 1.0 reads the byte or calls the view test, so wanderers, sounds and the rest don't depend on which chunks are drawn. The only other thing the choice drives: without the Expansion Pak, a neighbour that isn't drawn has its scenes freed when memory runs low (under `0x18000` free).
- **Where the original differs:** a copy of a scene starts from nothing when it's loaded, so neighbouring chunks' water is out of step in the original, by however far apart they were loaded. The runtime's copies also start together when their chunk loads, but it keeps each scene object's levels of detail for as long as the chunk is loaded. The original frees a level of detail out of range and loads it again later without the Expansion Pak, which restarts its water; with it, that isn't checked.

## Particles

The engine's particles ([particles](../formats/particles.md)) are drawn last in the frame, after the deferred scene objects. `ParticlesView` draws what the core's `ParticleHead` drew on its last frame, in the runtime and the editor.

- **The square** (`ParticleHead.Draw`, `0x800a92fc`): four vertices at ±353.55 × 16, scaled by the particle's scale × 0.001 and drawn at 16 to the unit, so a square 0.7071 × its scale on a side, centred on it. It faces along the camera's look and stays upright: one matrix a frame from `guAlign` of the look with no roll (`Particles.DrawPrologue`, `0x800a8f74`), which turns +Z back along the look, the same for every particle, not toward each one. The runtime turns each square by the camera's right and up, which are the same for a camera that doesn't roll. A particle with its own matrix (flag 0x4, the weather's splashes) is turned by that instead.
- **The texture** covers the square once, clamped, with its last row at the top (the vertices' T runs from the height down to 0), so the image shows upside down; the flames are stored that way up. It's filtered bilinearly, in one cycle, so with no mipmap levels.
- **The colour** is the particle's, each channel × `255.0f`, truncated and wrapped to a byte (`ColorBytes.Single`; a channel a fade has pushed negative wraps, `0x800a9480`), after × the screen's brightness when that's under 1, so particles darken with fades, alpha too. It's the vertices' shade: the combiner is `TEXEL0 × SHADE` for colour and alpha (`G_CC_MODULATERGBA`, `0xFC121824 FF33FFFF`), so an intensity texture gives its intensity as both. In on-screen values, as the N64 material is.
- **The blend** is `G_RM_ZB_XLU_SURF` (`0x504A50`): `pixel × a + screen × (1 − a)`, tested against the depth buffer and not written, with no fog, no culling and no lighting (geometry mode `0x200005`). Nothing is sorted: emitters draw in slot order and each one's particles newest first. The runtime draws each slot as one multimesh at a render priority of 100 and up, so after every other translucent draw, unsorted within it and in the original's order between slots.
- **How far.** 1.0 draws particles at any distance, unfogged, out to the far plane; the port draws an actor's only while the actor is drawn, at its alpha, the user's choice ([draw distance](#draw-distance)).
- **The first pass.** An emitter with flag 0x1000 is drawn after the centre chunk's scene instead, without the depth buffer, comparing the primitive's depth. No emitter the port runs has it, so the view doesn't draw one apart.
- **Between frames.** The core steps particles at 30 fps, as the original at that rate; the view moves each one on from where the frame before drew it to where the last did over the next two ticks, so they glide. The view does this itself every frame Godot draws, so its multimeshes must stay out of Godot's physics interpolation: that blends each instance from where the last physics tick left the same index, and an index is another particle once one is born or dies, which draws splashes for a frame where others had been.
- **Rain and snow** are two emitters of the same pool that the weather steers ([weather](weather.md#rain-and-snow)), drawn by the same view: streaks and flakes facing the camera, and splashes laid flat on the ground by their own orientation.

## Environment maps

A Borg2 display list with flag 0x1 has `G_TEXTURE_GEN` on: its texture coordinates come from its normals, for shiny metal such as the opening's door hinges. Addresses are from 1.0 and the RSP microcode it carries (F3DEX2 fifo 2.08).

- **The look-at** is made afresh before each such list (`BorgAnimDrawSceneRaw`, `0x80097a90`, `guLookAtReflect` at `0x800987f4`): from the scene's eye (`+0xF8`) at the node's world position, the translation row of its world matrix (`+0xF0` of the node's record, before the model's scale), with world up (0, 1, 0), into the RSP's look-at X and Y, in world space as the lights are. The look is eye − at, normalised; right is up × look, and the second axis look × right.
- **The scene's eye** is the translation row of its camera matrix (`+0xC8`), which `Borg5Scene.EvaluateNodes` (`0x80095518`) fills from the scene's own camera, transform 0, unless the scene has flag `0x40`. A cinematic's shot and the title screen's scenery keep their camera, so they look from its animated position, and the shading turns as the camera moves (`Cinematic.StartShot` sets only flag `0x10`). Trek's centre chunk's scene is given trek's camera (`SceneSetCameraLookAt`, `0x8009e1b4`, from `HandleZoneEngineFrame`). Neighbour chunks, scene objects and actors have flag `0x40` (`0x8009e444`), so they look from the world's origin, a direction that turns only as the node moves.
- **No eye at the node:** 1.0's `guLookAtReflectF` (`0x800b9b90`) doesn't guard its normalisation, and the game runs with the invalid-operation trap on (FPCSR `0x01000800`), so a list whose node sat on its eye would fault. The opening's door hinges sit exactly on the origin while the doors are shut, which is why the cinematic's eye can't be the origin.
- **The coordinates:** the normal after the model matrix, normalised, gives u = (1 + n · right) / 2 and v = (1 + n · up) / 2 across the whole texture (`G_TEXTURE` scales these lists by the texture's width and height, `W << 6` and `H << 6`, where others have `0x8000`). A normal facing up samples the texture's bottom row. The tile wraps or clamps as any list's does; the lit ones all wrap.
- **Without lighting there's no texgen:** the microcode generates coordinates only in its lighting path, so unlit lists use their own S and T, at that scale, 32,768 units across the texture. The one list with flag 0x2 (`G_TEXTURE_GEN_LINEAR`) is unlit, so the linear mode is never seen.
- **In the port** the export keeps these lists' textures, and the N64 material makes the coordinates from an instance's eye (`N64Material.TexGen`, `SetEye`), the world's origin unless set. A cinematic's view sets its camera's position each picture, which is exact.

## What's left

- **The lens flare's test** for what hides the sun uses the collision, not the depth buffer, so foliage doesn't hide it.
- **The projected sky under cameras the follow camera never is.** Cut-scene cameras can look straight up, and a few of the original's shots do. Three things break there, none of them in trek:
  - *Sprites flip overhead.* A patch is kept upright against the horizon by a right-hand side level with the ground and square to its heading. The sun passes straight overhead at noon, and the moon at 09:00 and 21:00. There the game turns the azimuth half round so the object carries on across the sky, which turns the patch's right-hand side round too, so the image turns 180° in one frame about its top-left corner: the sun's disc jumps by its own size, and the moon's crescent turns over. Taking the right-hand side from the azimuth before it turns would keep the image fixed to the sky. The clouds reach 53° at most, and the stars are points, so it's only the sun and moon.
  - *The camera is rebuilt from its look.* `SkyScreen.Project` gets only the way the camera looks, and assumes it's level from side to side (no roll) and 45° high. Looking exactly straight up or down draws no sprites at all. A camera that rolls leaves the sky upright while the scenery tilts, one that pitches over the top draws the sky upside down against the scenery, and one with another field of view, such as a cinematic shot's, unpins the sky from the scenery. The fix is to hand the core the camera's basis and field of view from Godot, not its look alone.
  - *The gradient's dome* is drawn from the camera's own basis and field of view, so it keeps up with any camera, but its top colour fills everything from 17° up: a cut-scene looking up sees a plain sky, where the original's squeezes the whole gradient onto the screen.
- **Texture filtering.** The N64's three-point filter isn't emulated. Aidyn doesn't use it for artistic effect, and its textures, the faces especially, have far more texels than the screen shows, so it's low priority. Side by side it shows only in cut-outs close up: the tree walls' edges, cut from a bilinear alpha, come out rounder and blobbier than the original's angular ones, while from a distance they match. The user left it.
- **A chunk scene's own translucent draws in front of bodies.** The original draws a chunk's scene, translucent lists and all, before the bodies, and its translucent draws don't write depth, so a body standing in them is drawn over them whole. Godot draws every translucent surface after the opaque ones (see *Order* under [the N64 material](#the-n64-material)), so they're blended over the body: in MiscInteriors C03 (scene `0x0D5E`), the misty sea's two translucent water layers wash Lycea's hem teal where the original's is dark. A chunk's second scene is drawn after the bodies in the original too, so only the first scene's translucent draws are concerned. One way: give bodies' materials a next pass in the transparent queue, after the chunk scenes' translucent draws and before the second scenes', that draws them again where their depth is equal.
- **Environment maps in trek.** A few chunk scenes, scene objects and actors have lit environment-mapped lists. The port looks at trek's from its world's origin, a map set's corner, where the original's is probably the centre chunk's, and the centre chunk's own scene should look from trek's camera ([environment maps](#environment-maps)). Settling the original's world frame for drawing would make them exact.
- **Which scenes are drawn**, apart from how fast textures scroll. The original's collision grid lists the scene objects to draw from each square (the player's in the centre chunk, and the squares along a neighbour's edge), and it draws a neighbour chunk, objects and all, only as [scrolling textures](#scrolling-textures) has it. The runtime draws all nine chunks and every object within its range, and leaves what's out of view to Godot.
- **The editor.** Billboards in its model view face the way they were modelled (its map set view turns them, as the runtime does). Opened and destroyed containers keep their closed model: the original swaps the linked scene object's model for its second or third, which the runtime does ([map-objects](map-objects.md#a-chunks-objects-at-run-time)), but the editor's **Flags** switch only leaves the container itself out.
